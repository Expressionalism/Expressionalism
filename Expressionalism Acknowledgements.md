# Acknowledgements

This document acknowledges the philosophical traditions, concepts, and formal resources that supplied generative starting points for Expressionalism. The framework is explicitly a synthesis.

Every notion and thinker below can be treated as a provisional secondary expression. None functions as a foundational authority, exhaustive truth, or final doctrine. Naming a source credits a starting point. No thinker named here is claimed to endorse the framework, and where the framework departs from a source, the entry says so. Expressionalism remains fallible: it draws selectively from these sources only insofar as they illuminate the P1–P8 presumptions, the Toolkit mechanics, or the mathematical model, then subjects them to the same certainty–uncertainty tension that every expression undergoes. The framework rests on its own internal coherence and provisional utility. Any particular look can fail in use (see A Note on Words), and a failed look is not a refuted framework.

## P1 – The Open Field and the Dilemma Gate

P1's Open Field is the widest synthesis space available once analysis of an expression has begun. It leans on relations as its method. It opens inquiry through a dilemma gate (Door 1 proceeds with relational contrasts available for further distinctions; Door 2 registers generative uncertainty or silence, with reciprocal uncertainty boosts) and protects non-relational gaps through operational mechanisms rather than ontological claims.

Gorgias of Leontini (c. 483–375 BCE) is reported by Sextus Empiricus to have argued in On Non-Existence the tripartite claim that nothing exists; if anything exists, it cannot be known; and if it can be known, it cannot be communicated. This radical sophistic performance serves as a test case for P1's Door 2: generative uncertainty and silence keep a place in the analysis beside relational construction, and the framework does not treat obtainability as given.

Heraclitus of Ephesus (c. 535–475 BCE) is credited with the sayings that one cannot step twice into the same river and that change is held together by an underlying logos. He supplies a picture of change that is also ordered, a starting point for P1's room for both relational construction and generative pauses without privileging stasis or fixed essence. The framework does not adopt a logos or any built-in order.

Laozi (Lao Tzu) presents the Dao in the Dao De Jing as the ineffable, effortless source that flows through and harmonizes all things without coercion or fixed essence. This non-forced, non-hierarchical openness offers a classical Eastern parallel to P1's ontologically permissive starting point and to the framework's refusal to give a teleological explanation for why expression occurs (see the Framework’s Introduction).

Nāgārjuna (c. 150–250 CE), founder of the Madhyamaka school and author of the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā, articulates the doctrine of śūnyatā (emptiness). All phenomena lack independent essence (svabhāva) and arise only through dependent origination (pratītyasamutpāda). Madhyamaka keeps a distinction between conventional and ultimate truth but does not take the ultimate to be a ground: it proceeds without positing any final ground, and treats emptiness itself as empty. This is an ancient case of refusing to fill in a final ground, which parallels P1's refusal to characterize what resists relational construction. The framework differs in one respect: it names a limit of obtainability, the Non-relational Remainder, without characterizing anything beyond it, and protects non-relational gaps operationally as a method, whereas Madhyamaka denies any final ground at all. The catuṣkoṭi, the fourfold form of affirming, negating, both, and neither that Nāgārjuna works with, is the form of the Toolkit's Tetralemma mapping, which the framework uses as a graded analytical tool and not as a doctrine.

Parmenides of Elea (c. 515–450 BCE) affirms in his poem On Nature that "it is" and that being is unchanging, complete, and one. This supplies the crisp affirmative pole for P1's dilemma gate. The framework takes the logical force of this affirmation as one pole and sets it beside Heraclitean flux and Nāgārjunian emptiness, so that the Open Field is not closed under any single ontology.

## P2 – Contrasting Parts in the Field

P2 registers that if analysis proceeds under P1's Door 1, contrasting parts may emerge as degrees of difference among secondary expressions; if secondary expressions remain undifferentiated or uniform, the pointing-aspect stays minimally differentiated and the expression registers as minimally usable for further analysis.

Henri Bergson (1859–1941) develops the concepts of durée (duration) as continuous, qualitative becoming and élan vital as creative impetus toward novelty. Durée models contrasts that remain fluid and processual rather than static binaries, a starting point for P2's spectrum of contrast and for treating differences as dynamic. The framework does not take over élan vital: it gives no teleological explanation for why expression occurs.

Gilles Deleuze (1925–1995) and Félix Guattari (1930–1992) describe assemblages and rhizomes as centerless, proliferating networks of connections without hierarchical origin or terminus. These offer an image of P2's contrasting parts as open, non-hierarchical webs capable of generating multiplicity without collapse into uniformity.

Alfred North Whitehead (1861–1947) articulates in Process and Reality an ontology in which actual occasions prehend one another into novel unities. This supplies a relational, non-substantial model for the active, processual contrasts that appear in analysis. Prehensions function as the basic "feelings" or grasps through which contrasts become usable for further construction. The framework takes the relational picture without adopting Whitehead's ontology.

Gregory Bateson (1904–1980) developed the cybernetic insight that information is "a difference which makes a difference." This parallels P2's contrasting parts, the differences among secondary expressions that carry an analysis forward. Bateson's emphasis on context, pattern, and the relational nature of information also resonates with the Toolkit's resonance, stability, and alignment readouts.

## P3 – Relational Construction of Contrasting Entities

P3 registers that contrasting parts undergo relational construction: they bifurcate into connectable relational paths or unconnectable non-relational gaps; the connectable paths co-emerge with repeatability (high invariants, mid sequences, low uniques); and decomposition yields passive relateds and active relators operating in self-pointing or other-pointing mode. The Non-relational Remainder names the limit of obtainability. Isolation Residual (isolation density) rises when pointing is refused; neither it nor the Remainder is a third peer mode, and Isolation Residual is not the Remainder. Synthesis Refusal is the label for a listed support whose traction is refused, kept out of aggregation and distributions.

Early Buddhist teaching on dependent origination holds that phenomena arise in dependence on conditions, and Nāgārjuna's Madhyamaka extends this to the claim that nothing has independent essence. This classical account of conditioned arising parallels P3's relational construction and the framework's refusal to treat any element as self-sufficient. The operational protection of non-relational gaps, which resists relational creep, is the framework's own default heuristic, not a Buddhist doctrine.

Charles Sanders Peirce (1839–1914) develops a triadic semiotics of sign (representamen), object, and interpretant. The triad is a historical neighbor to P3's decomposition into relateds and relators, though it has three terms where the framework has two functional roles that can shift under P8. His pragmatic maxim, that a concept's content lies in its conceivable practical effects, is a starting point for the stance in A Note on Words that a look is testable in use, which is to say it can fail in use. His abduction, the inference that adopts an explanatory hypothesis, is the starting point for the Toolkit's Secondary Abduction and its optional Peircean Abduction Heuristic. Critique 6 names Peircean semiotics among the sources the framework synthesizes; like every source named in this document, he is not a foundational authority.

Ferdinand de Saussure (1857–1913) articulates a differential system of signs in which what a sign is depends on its differences from other signs rather than on positive content. This supports P3's treatment of every element as defined through its relations and reinforces the framework's refusal to treat any element as self-sufficient. The framework applies the relational view to any expression, not to language alone.

Ludwig Wittgenstein (1889–1951) concludes the Tractatus with the remark, in the Pears and McGuinness translation, "What we cannot speak about we must pass over in silence," and in his later work treats meaning as use within language games. The Tractatus remark parallels the Non-relational Remainder as a limit that is named and not filled. Synthesis Refusal is the Toolkit's own operational label, not Wittgenstein's. The framework's limit is a limit of obtainability, not a limit of language, and it makes no claim about what lies beyond it.

Susanne Langer (1895–1985) distinguishes between discursive symbolism (language and logic) and presentational symbolism (art, music, and the articulation of feeling and lived experience). This distinction supports the framework's capacity to treat different modes of expression—linguistic, phenomenal, and artistic—without forcing all pointing into propositional form, while preserving the tensive ratio between the connectable certainties and the uncertainties and gaps that remain.

Ernst Cassirer (1874–1945), in his Philosophy of Symbolic Forms, treats symbolic formation (myth, language, science, and art) as fundamental ways in which experience and the world of objects are formed. His account of symbolic expression as an active, world-forming process offers a broad philosophical parallel to the framework's treatment of expression as its fundamental primitive and to the two modes, self-pointing and other-pointing, through which pointing occurs. The framework does not adopt his neo-Kantian view that symbolic forms constitute reality.

## P4 – Layering in Expressions

P4 registers that relateds and relators organize into hierarchical expressions—subatomic (singles), atomic (sequences), and complex (nests)—while remaining open to perceptual role shifts under P8.

Aristotle (384–322 BCE) develops hylomorphism, in which things are composites of matter (hylē) shaped by form (morphē). This offers a classical precedent for thinking of wholes as organized from parts, a starting point for P4's layered expressions, without committing the framework to substance metaphysics.

Plotinus (204–270 CE) describes reality through emanation in hierarchical levels from the One. This provides a provisional model for P4's layering spectrum, while the framework does not adopt any fixed ontological priority or teleological necessity.

Whitehead also supplies, in his account of societies and nexuses of actual occasions, a processual, non-substantial basis for hierarchical layering that is compatible with P8 perceptual renewal and Functional Role Shift.

## P5 – Plurality of Expressions

P5 registers that layered expressions manifest as a current primary expression co-existing with provisional secondary expressions on a spectrum of multiplicity (solitary, finite, or infinite).

William James (1842–1910) and John Dewey (1859–1952), developing the pragmatism that Peirce began, tie the meaning of ideas to their consequences in lived contexts; James adds a pluralist outlook, and Dewey the testing of ideas in inquiry. This is a starting point for P5's co-existence of primary and secondary expressions and for P5's relational calibration, which proceeds without presuming independence or substrates. The framework takes their practical orientation, not either thinker's theory of truth.

Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1646–1716) articulates in the Monadology a plurality of simple substances, each mirroring the whole from its own perspective. This illustrates the idea of many perspectives on one whole. The framework does not take over his simple substances, his denial of real relations between them, or his pre-established harmony.

Willard Van Orman Quine (1908–2000) advances ontological relativity and the indeterminacy of translation, arguing that what a term refers to is relative to a background language or theory. This supports P5's treatment of any secondary expression as relative to a background. Quine himself did not conclude that ontology is optional.

## P6 – Comparability and Measurement of Expressions

P6 registers that plurality enables a spectrum of alignability and transduction, producing distances and divergences that can be measured as utilities.

Rudolf Carnap (1891–1970) develops logical empiricism, including a verificationist criterion that ties meaningful statements to experience and logical structure and that Carnap later softened into confirmation. He is credited here by contrast. The framework uses no criterion of meaningfulness; it extends comparison equitably to phenomenal and transduced inputs.

Information-theoretic divergences, particularly the Jensen-Shannon divergence (symmetric) and the Kullback-Leibler divergence (asymmetric), shape the Toolkit's Alignment, Resonance, and directedness-differential readouts. These support the quantification of relational connectivity without reducing meaning to information.

## P7 – Evaluation and Yield

P7 aggregates certainties and uncertainties into provisional dialectical yields (balanced, tensive, tempered, or raw). The yield spectrum itself encodes self-reflexive evaluation.

G.W.F. Hegel (1770–1831) supplies the dialectical spirit of progress through negation and sublation. The framework takes this spirit as a starting point for P7's certainty–uncertainty aggregation, stripped of teleological necessity and of any claim to final synthesis.

Charles Sanders Peirce also pairs abduction with permanent fallibilism. His fallibilism is a starting point for P7's provisional yields, including the Claim-Tempered variant. Peirce expected inquiry to converge; the framework makes no such claim.

Karl Popper (1902–1994) advances falsificationism and critical rationalism. These shape P7's yield adjudication, the Toolkit's Falsify Mode, and the commitment to testable provisionality. The framework's "falsifiable" is narrower than Popper's: as A Note on Words defines it, a look can fail in use.

## P8 – Perception and Orientation: The Renewal Loop

P8 registers that once a yield is reached, perception and orientation return to the user the question of what comes next: the yield stands as provisionally accepted (P8 Door 1), or it opens to any form of re-expression, refinement, modular incorporation, or outright discard (P8 Door 2). It functions as the renewal mechanism of the schema.

Nāgārjuna's Madhyamaka also offers a middle way through negation of extremes without final closure. This parallels the P8 Gate and the open renewal loop.

Pyrrho of Elis (c. 360–c. 270 BCE), and the Pyrrhonian skeptics who followed him, model epochē (suspension of judgment) as a pause that keeps the P8 Gate available rather than prematurely closed.

Heinz von Foerster (1911–2002) develops second-order cybernetics, in which systems observe themselves observing. This is a starting point for P8's reflexive renewal and for the Toolkit's Phase 5 certainty tempering, in which the Toolkit raises doubts against its own certainties from the P1–P8 starting points they leave unaddressed.

Hans-Georg Gadamer (1900–2002) articulates the event of understanding as a "fusion of horizons," in which the interpreter's horizon and the horizon of the text or phenomenon are brought into productive tension and transformed. This offers a parallel to P8's perception gate, where re-expression and renewal arise through encounter rather than through the application of fixed criteria, and where the act of understanding itself modifies the pointing under examination.

## Formal, Mathematical, and Computational Resources

The Unified Formal Model, Computational Substrate, and Toolkit operationalize the P1–P8 distinctions through continuous spectra, hybrid functions, normalized Bayesian procedures, dilemma gates, tetralemma and pentalemma mappings, and metrics including the Secondary Coherence Score and Inferred Relator Mode Distribution. These technical resources draw upon several formal traditions.

Lotfi Zadeh (1921–2017) introduces fuzzy set theory and fuzzy membership functions. These are the starting point for the membership function μ(x) in the hybrid function H(x) = min(1, μ(x) × Pₙₒᵣₘ(x | V)), in its linear and clamped sigmoid forms, as employed across the presumptions and Toolkit phases. Fuzzy approaches enable graded, non-binary distinctions that align with the framework's commitment to continuous spectra rather than sharp ontological cuts.

Bayesian epistemology and normalized posterior procedures provide the mechanism for updating under uncertainty. The normalized Bayesian posterior supports the abduction of secondary expressions in Phase 2 and the computation of traction, while remaining compatible with the framework's fallibilism and balanced sampling.

Claude Shannon (1916–2001) establishes information theory and entropy as measures of uncertainty and surprise. Shannon's entropy is a starting point for the divergence measures behind the Toolkit's alignment, resonance, and related readouts. The framework employs these measures diagnostically; a score is not meaning (see A Note on Words).

Constantino Tsallis (born 1943) proposes in 1988 (Journal of Statistical Physics 52, 479) a generalization of entropy governed by a parameter q; an earlier form of the same expression is due to Havrda and Charvát (1967). Computational Substrate §4.6 evaluates it on the same normalized distribution used for classical Avg Density, at a fixed default q = 0.9, as a supporting reading, the Hybrid Non-Extensive Density, that never enters the Inferred Relator Mode Distribution or the Secondary Coherence Score. This is a credit for a method; the framework takes the formula as a reading instrument and makes no claim about non-extensive physics.

The cybernetic tradition, initiated by Norbert Wiener (1894–1964) and extended into second-order cybernetics by Heinz von Foerster (credited under P8), supplies conceptual resources for reflexive, circular, and self-observing systems.

Kurt Gödel (1906–1978) proved in his incompleteness theorems that any consistent, effectively axiomatized formal system that can express basic arithmetic contains statements it can neither prove nor refute. This is credited as an analogy. A framework that analyzes itself can meet limits it cannot settle from within, and the analogy is to the limit the Non-relational Remainder names and to Synthesis Refusal, where a run loses traction. The analogy is loose: the theorems concern formal provability, not obtainability, and the framework registers gaps operationally rather than making positive ontological claims.

Alfred Tarski (1901–1983) shows in his work on truth that a sufficiently rich formal language cannot define its own truth predicate (the liar paradox shows why), so truth for such a language is defined in a metalanguage, which separates object language from metalanguage. He is credited here by contrast. The framework does not adopt that separation of levels: it treats its own analyses as expressions that the same schema can examine, and it accepts the resulting circularity as a consequence of remaining non-dogmatic about its own status, since no system can fully step outside itself (see Critiques 1 and 21). The framework offers no definition of truth for its own analyses; it treats a score as the quantitative state of a look, not as truth taken whole (see A Note on Words).

The digital physics and computational ontology tradition, including Konrad Zuse, Edward Fredkin, Stephen Wolfram, and Seth Lloyd, along with Melvin Vopson's proposed second law of infodynamics (2022–2026), supplies secondary expressions that a look can take up on questions such as the Simulation Hypothesis. A run marks how these ideas hold on a look; it is not evidence that the world is, or is not, computed. When computational or energetic limits exceed current relational traction, the framework registers them explicitly as Synthesis Refusal.

### Systems and Cybernetics

Gregory Bateson (credited under P2) and Heinz von Foerster (credited under P8) also belong to this group.

Gilbert Simondon (1924–1989) articulates a philosophy of individuation centered on transduction—the relational operation by which structure and organization propagate from one domain or element to another. His realism of relations, in which relations are constitutive rather than secondary to pre-existing substances, offers a parallel to P3's relational construction and P6's spectrum of transduction and alignability. The framework uses "transduction" in a narrower, operational sense: the conversion of raw input into relational secondary expressions.

Niklas Luhmann (1927–1998) develops a theory of autopoietic social systems built from communication and the system/environment difference, with second-order observation as a central concept. His account of systems that observe their own observing operations offers a parallel to P8's renewal loop and to Phase 5 of the Toolkit, in which the Toolkit raises doubts against its own certainties from the P1–P8 starting points they leave unaddressed. Luhmann's distinction between system and environment also loosely resonates with the framework's operational separation between relational construction and protected non-relational gaps.

Humberto Maturana (1928–2021) and Francisco Varela (1946–2001) develop the concepts of autopoiesis and structural coupling. Autopoiesis models living systems as self-producing networks that are organizationally closed yet open to matter and energy, loosely paralleling the framework's treatment of secondary synthesis as the engine through which primary expressions obtain. Structural coupling describes how systems and their environments change together through recurrent interaction, offering a biological and cognitive parallel to P3's relational construction and to the Toolkit's emphasis on pointing as enacted relation.

These resources do not constitute foundational axioms. They function as operational tools and parallels whose outputs remain subject to P7 dialectical adjudication and P8 renewal.
